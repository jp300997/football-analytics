Australia Board

Eleven Socceroos and Matildas matches from the 2022 and 2023 World Cups, built on StatsBomb 360: event data plus the measured position of every player the broadcast camera could see.

Open board

The other boards here have no positional data at all, so this one is used for two things. First, to show the Australian sides' shape as it actually was: pick where the ball was, and the pitch shows where players really stood, averaged over every validated freeze frame with the ball in that zone. Second, to put an error bar on every positional claim the other boards make.

That second use is the point. Scored against measured truth, an event-only position estimate is accurate to about 5.7 m for the player on the ball — 4.3 m when the estimate is only a few seconds old, degrading to 27 m once it is reaching a minute. For off-ball players it is 21 m at best, against 29 m for assuming all eleven stand on the centre spot. Positional data is not a nicer version of event data. It answers a question event data cannot reach, and this board says so with a number.

  • Campaigns. Both tournaments match by match, with real formation events and in-match shifts, reconciled to the real scorelines.
  • Measured shape. Player density by ball zone, each grid cell carrying its own camera-visibility denominator.
  • Event data vs 360. Positional estimate error, what the under_pressure flag is worth in metres, and whether a pass went into space.
  • Australians. Every Australian who played, with the measured space each had on the ball and how often an opponent was inside five metres.
  • Pathway. Where A-League minutes go by age and nationality, across both competitions and two seasons.

Block height and block width are not computed anywhere on this board. The camera follows the ball, so any average of visible defenders' positions measures where the camera pointed as much as where the defence stood.

EPL Tactics Board

Seven views of how each club builds, presses, shapes up and attacks set pieces, and a Matchup tab that pits any club's build-up against any other club's pressing.

Open board

Every number on the board comes from real events, and every sequence is modelled rather than replayed. Passing chains and press episodes are stored as tries of real transitions with low-support branches pruned, so "most likely" walks the tree deterministically and "one real draw" samples it. Small-sample caveats sit in the captions, not the footnotes.

  • Passing. Build-up by zone and channel, progressive outlets vs recycling anchors, sequence lengths, long-ball share.
  • Pressing. Press episodes from possession spells: PPDA by third, block height, regain speed, coordination, intensity by game state.
  • Formations. Real Opta formation events and in-game shape changes, not shapes inferred from average positions.
  • Set Pieces. Corners and free kicks plotted at real delivery and shot coordinates, with takers and first-contact defenders.
  • Players. Full squad profiles, player-vs-player duels resolved through Opta's linked-event qualifiers, minutes and starts.
  • Matchup. Attacker's build-up share × defender's regain multiplier per zone, ranked Exploit / Contested / Danger, with a stated approach.

Opposition Dossiers

Analysis Hub

The dossiers are one page in a family. Every board reads the same way: the conclusion first in plain words, the reason one click away, one colour rule, and confidence stated with the sample rather than as a p-value.

Open the hub

The house style is a written spec the boards are built and reviewed against, because earlier versions were rejected three times for the same reasons: too dense, assuming the reader knew why, charts that could not be read at once. The rules are hard requirements now. A default view is at most about 350 visible words. Every flag carries a plain-language reason with the number, the comparison and the sample. Every chart states its conclusion in its title. No radar charts.

Post-match is a board in its own right. Match review asks what happened against the plan, and who was taken out of the game and how, rather than restating the scoreline. The most undeserved result of the season so far was Sunderland 3-5 Man City, a Sunderland win 64% of the time on the chances created.

Calibration in public · EPL + Champions League 2026/27

A model that keeps its own score

A forecasting model is here as a way of proving a habit: predictions logged before kick-off, scored automatically once results land, and published whether or not they flatter the model.

The habit is the transferable part. Every unattended run writes its pre-kick-off numbers to a prediction log. When results arrive, the last snapshot before kick-off is the one scored, so there is no way to quietly re-fit and claim the better number afterwards. The same discipline is what makes a player-availability model or a talent-ID shortlist worth trusting a year later: not that it was right, but that the claim was written down, timestamped and graded against a benchmark someone else set.

The benchmark here is published closing prices, which is simply the hardest public forecast of a football result available. A backtest fit blind to a full season put that benchmark ahead of the raw model, and the live record so far agrees, so the model is used as one input beside it rather than instead of it. That result is on the page because it is the result, and a record that is only published when it wins is not a record.

Underneath: team ratings are per-season nodes with club-specific carry-over, a squad-value prior and hierarchical shrinkage across league groups. Injuries scrape from Transfermarkt into per-player multipliers, fatigue reads from fixture congestion, and each match is simulated minute by minute. The injury and fatigue layers are availability and load modelling by another name.

Working principle

Never show a coach more precision than the data supports.

Event data without tracking tells you what happened on the ball. It does not tell you where the other twenty players stood. Every choice in these tools follows from that limit — and on the Australia board, where 360 frames exist, that limit is measured rather than asserted.

  • Positional estimates for off-ball players are tiered as interpolated, recent or estimated, and labelled as such on the pitch. Scored against measured positions, that estimate is worth about 5.7 m for the player on the ball and 21 m at best for everyone else.
  • Formations come from the provider's own formation events. Inferring shape from average positions was tried and dropped, and the error above is why.
  • Indirect free kicks are not linked to a resulting shot, because no qualifier connects them and a time-window guess would be a fabrication.
  • Block height and block width are not computed from 360 frames, because the camera follows the ball and the number would partly measure the camera.
  • Backtests and the live record are published even where they favour the benchmark.

Given tracking and physical data, the first three things I would build are the off-ball runs event data cannot see at all, a pressing-line-height measure that is honest because full-pitch tracking has no camera bias to correct for, and a load-to-availability link that turns the fixture-congestion proxy in the forecasting model into something measured. The Australia board is the argument for all three, in the only form I can make it from open data.

Pipelines, data quality and CI

How it is kept honest

Every push to this repository re-asserts the published numbers against their invariants and re-checks the published pages. If a board stops reconciling, the build fails rather than the reader finding out.

Read the pipeline

The raw event feed is 102 MB and is not committed, so CI cannot re-run the pipeline. What it can do is re-assert that the published numbers still hold together, which is the failure mode that actually matters: a refactor that quietly changes a denominator, a threshold that stops being applied, a share that creeps above 100%. 997 invariant checks run on the committed outputs, plus publication checks over every page. Both scripts are dependency-free, so the whole run needs no install step.

Most of those checks exist because the thing they defend was once wrong. Each one is commented with the bug it came from, so the suite reads as a record of what this data does when you are not looking:

  • Goals must reconcile to the real scoreline. The France shootout was being counted as ten goals and roughly seven xG, because shootout penalties are ordinary shot events. The Enzo Fernández own goal was missing, because own goals are their own event type and carry no xG.
  • Minutes must reconcile to eleven times full time. A tactical shift opens a second, overlapping lineup row for the same player, and one of those rows claimed a player was still on the pitch twelve minutes after she was substituted. Minutes are derived from the event stream instead.
  • The xG parts must sum to the whole. Throw-ins were being classified as set pieces, putting half the Matildas' xG in the wrong bucket.
  • Every density cell must carry its own visibility denominator. Dividing by the frame count instead reports an empty pitch wherever the camera did not look.
  • A stale position estimate cannot beat a fresh one. A monotonicity check on the error-by-staleness curve, which would catch the interpolation or the staleness bookkeeping breaking.
  • No dead links, and no vocabulary from the unpublished market-facing boards. This one caught a live broken link on the published site: the opposition board carries the shared board navigation, and one entry pointed at a board that is deliberately not published here.

Two checks of mine failed on correct data while this was being written, and both were the check being wrong rather than the pipeline: starts multiplied by ninety is not a lower bound on minutes, because starters get substituted. Those are in the commit history too.
